# Learning Psychology Strategies

This document describes psychology-based learning mechanics for the gamified 7th grade math app. The goal is to make students pay closer attention, practice longer, remember more, and feel capable while they work through TEKS-aligned math skills.

## Video-To-Hint Pipeline

Each problem type should have a short instructional video that shows the full solution in small parts. The app can use still frames from that video as hints.

Recommended structure:

- Part 1: Read and interpret the problem.
- Part 2: Choose a strategy or model.
- Part 3: Set up the work.
- Part 4: Solve step by step.
- Part 5: Check the answer.

If a student gets the problem wrong after hints, the app can require a quick rewatch of the specific video segment tied to the missed step. This should feel like targeted coaching, not punishment.

Example:

- Student misses `-3 - 5`.
- App identifies the likely issue: direction on the number line.
- Student rewatches the 12-second clip showing "subtract means move left."
- Student retries a similar problem immediately.

## Attention Strategy: Watch-To-Unlock

Students can unlock a practice level only after watching the example once or passing a short "skip check."

Options:

- Watch the video to unlock practice.
- Answer a one-question check to skip the video.
- Rewatch only the part connected to the missed step.

This respects students who already know the skill while nudging others to attend to the example.

## Retrieval Practice

Students should repeatedly pull information from memory instead of only rereading or rewatching.

Mechanics:

- After a video, ask one quick recall question.
- After a hint, ask the student to choose the next step.
- After a correct answer, ask a "why did this work?" micro-question.
- After a wrong answer, give a similar retry problem instead of moving on.

Example:

"You used `10 x 7 + 2 x 7` to solve `12 x 7`. What expression would help with `11 x 7`?"

## Spaced Review

The app should bring skills back over time so students do not forget them after one unit.

Review schedule:

- Same day: retry missed skills.
- Next day: one short review quest.
- Three days later: mixed practice.
- One week later: boss battle review.
- Three weeks later: spiral challenge.

The game can present this as "old enemies returning stronger."

## Interleaving

Students should mix related problem types after initial practice. This teaches them to choose strategies instead of memorizing the pattern from the last problem.

Example mixed set:

- Integer addition
- Integer subtraction
- Subtracting a negative
- Multiplying signed numbers
- Word problem with negative change

Interleaving should happen after the student has had enough focused practice to avoid confusion.

## Productive Struggle

The app should let students think before helping, then provide support before frustration takes over.

Mechanics:

- Wait briefly before showing hints.
- Offer visual hints before direct procedural hints.
- Let students request help anytime.
- Give guided solve mode after repeated struggle.
- Celebrate corrected answers.

Good message:

"That one had a trap in it. Let's rebuild it together."

Avoid:

"Incorrect again."

## Error Analysis

Students learn a lot by diagnosing mistakes.

Problem type:

- Show a fake student's work.
- Ask what went wrong.
- Ask how to fix it.
- Then ask the student to solve a similar problem.

Example:

```text
Problem: -3 - 5
Fake work: -3 - 5 = 2
Question: What mistake did this student make?
```

Expected insight:

The student moved right instead of left.

## Confidence Calibration

Before submitting, ask students how confident they are.

Options:

- Not sure
- Pretty sure
- Locked in

Use this privately to improve feedback.

Examples:

- Correct and "not sure": give encouragement and a short explanation.
- Incorrect and "locked in": show contrastive feedback because the misconception may be strong.
- Correct and "locked in": give faster progression.

## Immediate Correction Loop

When students miss a problem, they should correct it right away.

Recommended loop:

1. Show what part went wrong.
2. Show the relevant video still or clip.
3. Ask a smaller step question.
4. Give a similar problem.
5. Award full XP when corrected.

This turns wrong answers into a path forward instead of a dead end.

## Variable Rewards

Students should sometimes receive surprise rewards for good learning behaviors.

Reward triggers:

- Correcting a missed problem.
- Rewatching a targeted clip.
- Completing a review quest.
- Beating a personal best.
- Helping their team leaderboard.
- Returning to a previously weak skill.

Rewards can include coins, cosmetics, XP bursts, title badges, or fake ad skips.

Use variable rewards carefully. They should reinforce learning behaviors, not create pressure to grind endlessly.

## Commitment Device

Before starting a challenge, students can choose a small goal.

Examples:

- "I will complete 5 problems."
- "I will earn 2 stars."
- "I will beat my last score."
- "I will use a hint instead of guessing."

After the level, the app shows whether they met the goal.

This makes students more likely to persist because they made the goal themselves.

## Loss Aversion Without Punishment

Avoid taking away earned progress. Instead, use temporary opportunities.

Good examples:

- "Double XP is active for the next 10 minutes."
- "Your streak shield expires after today."
- "This boss ticket refreshes tomorrow."

Avoid:

- Removing coins for wrong answers.
- Lowering rank for hint use.
- Publicly showing failure streaks.

## Fresh Start Effect

Use new days, weeks, seasons, and units as reset moments.

Mechanics:

- Weekly leaderboard reset.
- New battle pass season.
- New unit theme.
- "Monday reset quest."
- "New world unlocked."

This helps students who fell behind feel like they can re-enter the game.

## Self-Explanation

Occasionally ask students to explain their thinking.

Lightweight formats:

- Choose the correct explanation.
- Fill one blank in a sentence.
- Drag steps into order.
- Record a short written explanation for teacher review.

Example:

"I moved left on the number line because I was subtracting a positive number."

## Mastery Identity

Students should earn identity-based titles that reinforce growth.

Examples:

- Integer Navigator
- Ratio Builder
- Percent Strategist
- Equation Solver
- Data Detective
- Probability Planner
- Budget Boss

These titles should be tied to demonstrated mastery, improvement, or persistence.

## Social Belonging

Middle school students often engage more when progress feels shared.

Mechanics:

- Class-wide boss health bar.
- Team quests.
- Anonymous class goals.
- "The class solved 1,000 rational number problems this week."
- Cooperative reward chests.

This lets lower-performing students contribute without being exposed.

## Desirable Difficulty

The app should gradually increase difficulty when students are ready.

Difficulty levers:

- Larger numbers
- Negative values
- Fractions instead of whole numbers
- Multi-step problems
- Word problem context
- Mixed review
- Timed mode
- Less scaffolding

The app should reduce difficulty temporarily after repeated errors, then build back up.

## Metacognition Prompts

Students should occasionally reflect on strategy, not just answer.

Examples:

- "Which hint helped most?"
- "What made this problem tricky?"
- "Did you use a model, mental math, or equation?"
- "Would you like more like this or a challenge?"

This teaches students to notice how they learn.

## Recommended Rule

The strongest psychology strategy for this app is:

Make the fastest path through the game also be the best learning path.

That means the game should reward students for watching carefully, using hints, correcting mistakes, reviewing old skills, and retrying hard problems. If those behaviors earn XP, unlock currency, cosmetics, and leaderboard progress, students will naturally do the things that help them learn.
